How accuracy is tracked

Every call is logged and checked against what actually happened

Most AI research tools hand you a read and move on. This one logs each call: the stated read, the confidence level, and the price at the time. Later, it is checked against the real, realized price move using public market data. If stated confidence has not actually tracked being right, that result is reported too. A dated, sourced record of why a name was researched also serves as a record of diligence performed, which has value beyond the moment of the call.

Self-grading: plain math, not AI

A real, checkable track record, building on your own watchlist

Every logged call is scored against what the stock actually did, split out by how confident the call said it was, so you can see directly whether higher stated confidence has meant more often right, or whether it hasn't. This isn't marketed as "self-improving," a claim no LLM can honestly make about itself. This is accountability to real outcomes you can audit yourself, which is different, and more useful.

Calibration audit: where AI re-enters, bounded

Finding its own blind spots, never a trading rule

Once there's enough real, graded history on your names, Claude analyzes its own track record directly, not a person guessing at patterns by hand. The output has no field for "what to do next" or a return estimate, only descriptive findings about this tool's own past accuracy, each required to cite its real sample size. It declines to run at all below a real minimum sample, since a pattern found in too few outcomes is noise, not a finding, and the tool is built to know the difference.

How the AI part actually works

Two layers. Only one of them is AI.

Here's exactly what "powered by AI" means: the AI does the reading and writing. The checking is just math.

Layer 1: Checking

Plain math, not AI

Scoring every past read against the real price move that followed, using free market data. Split out by how confident the call said it was, so you can see directly whether higher stated confidence has actually meant more often right. Same input, same answer, every time. No model makes this call.

Layer 2: Research

Where the AI actually works

Claude reads the filings and the news, searches systematically rather than stopping at the first plausible result, and turns what it finds into a structured read: a sentiment, a confidence level, and the actual sources it used. It's fast. It can also sound confident while it's wrong, which is exactly why it never skips the layer above.

Built, not just promised

Tested hard enough to find real problems before anyone else could

Every one of these was caught in our own testing, before a result ever reached anyone outside this project. None of it was something a customer ever saw or was affected by. It's shown here because a tool that has never found a real problem in itself usually just hasn't looked hard enough.

Caught in testing: Hedging instead of deciding Fixed and confirmed same day

Three different stocks, the same evasive non-answer

Three internal test runs on three unrelated tickers all independently landed on "mixed, confidence 0.55." Not because the evidence actually tied, but because the prompt only told the model when to lower confidence, never when to commit. Fixed with one instruction: decide which way the preponderance of evidence leans, and reserve "mixed" for genuine toss-ups. Confirmed against four follow-up names before trusting it.

Caught at: internal testing Fix confirmed on: 4 follow-up names
Caught in testing: A read that kept flip-flopping Fixed before anyone relied on it

Stopping at the first plausible search gave an unstable read

An early test run on a biotech name with a pending FDA decision changed direction across repeat runs, because the research step stopped as soon as it had enough to write something plausible and missed the actual FDA briefing document that would have changed the read. Fixed by requiring a systematic search for primary documents, and an active search for the strongest evidence against the current read, before concluding anything.

Caught at: internal testing Fix confirmed on: 4 unrelated situations
Caught in testing: A cost ceiling that wasn't there Fixed same day, verified on re-run

A pending-acquisition name found a gap in the budget

Testing against a company with an announced buyout exposed a real gap: asking for an ordinary analyst price target on a name like that is a question without a clean answer, so the research step searched further than it should have. Fixed twice the same day, with a guard that recognizes a pending deal and redirects to the real number (the deal spread), plus a hard ceiling so no single request can run past a few minutes, on any tool, for any reason. Verified fixed on a live re-run.
